Emergency Pet Care: What to Do When Your Pet Sitter Can't Help

A practical emergency response guide for pet owners away from home when their usual sitter cannot help.

You are 300 miles from home when your phone rings. Your pet sitter has a family emergency and cannot be there tonight. Or perhaps your sitter has found your dog unwell and cannot reach your vet. Emergencies involving pets are stressful at any time; they are significantly more stressful when you are not there to manage them directly. Preparation is the only reliable solution.

The Emergency Plan You Need Before You Leave

Every time you leave your pet with a sitter, they should have a written emergency plan, not just your phone number. This plan should be physically present in your home (not just sent digitally) and cover the following:

  • Primary vet details: Name, address, phone number, and your account number or reference. Include hours of operation so the sitter knows when to use the emergency line instead.
  • Nearest 24-hour emergency animal hospital: Address, phone number, and approximate driving time from your home. Research this before you leave, not when the emergency happens.
  • Your contact details: Cell phone, hotel or destination contact, and a secondary contact (family member or close friend) who can make decisions if you are unreachable.
  • Pet medical summary: Current medications and doses, known allergies, microchip number, insurance provider and policy number, and any pre-existing conditions the treating vet should know about.
  • Signed authorization letter: A written, signed statement authorizing the sitter to seek and approve emergency veterinary treatment up to a specified spending limit without your prior approval.
  • Backup care contacts: At least two people who could take over pet care at short notice if the primary sitter becomes unavailable.

What to Do Right Now If Your Sitter Cannot Come

If your sitter cancels and you cannot get home, work through this sequence:

  1. Call your backup sitter or backup care contacts immediately. Do not assume they are available; call and confirm.
  2. Contact a professional pet sitting service (Rover, Wag, local equivalents) for same-day or emergency bookings. Many platforms have sitters available with several hours notice.
  3. Contact friends, neighbors, or family members who know your pet and could check in or take over short-term care.
  4. Contact your veterinary clinic. Many practices can board patients in emergencies, even outside normal boarding capacity, and this option is underused by owners in crisis.
  5. Call local pet boarding facilities directly. Explain it is an emergency situation. Many will accommodate same-day boarders when their normal advance-booking policy would normally prevent it.

Managing a Pet Medical Emergency Remotely

If your pet is showing signs of illness or injury while you are away, these steps apply whether or not your regular sitter is available:

  1. Assess urgency first. Call your vet or emergency clinic and describe the symptoms to a professional. They will advise whether this requires immediate emergency attendance, a same-day appointment, or monitoring at home.
  2. Instruct your sitter clearly. Give specific instructions: which clinic to go to, what to say when they arrive, and that you have authorized treatment. Call ahead to the clinic yourself to confirm authorization.
  3. Stay available. Give your sitter or the treating vet your phone number with a request to call immediately on any updates, and keep your phone on during the treatment period.
  4. Follow up with your own vet for ongoing care once you return home, even if emergency treatment was provided elsewhere.

Pet Insurance: The Underrated Emergency Tool

Pet insurance removes one of the most agonizing dimensions of a remote emergency: the financial decision. When a sitter is told that stabilizing a dog will cost $3,000 and they cannot reach you, they should not be in a position of declining treatment because of cost uncertainty. An active pet insurance policy with a known annual limit and deductible, combined with a clear authorization letter, empowers your sitter to act without hesitation.

Note on Poison Emergencies If your pet has ingested a potentially toxic substance, time is critical. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center operates 24 hours a day at (888) 426-4435. Give your sitter this number in their written instructions. Many poisoning cases are treatable if managed within the first 1 to 2 hours, and significantly harder to treat after the absorption window has passed.

What counts as a pet emergency?+
True emergencies requiring immediate veterinary attention include: difficulty breathing, suspected poisoning, seizures, collapse or inability to stand, suspected bloat (swollen abdomen with unproductive retching in dogs), uncontrolled bleeding, eye injuries, inability to urinate (especially in male cats and dogs), suspected broken bones, severe vomiting or diarrhea with blood, extreme lethargy, and pale or blue gums. When in doubt, call your emergency vet and describe the symptoms for triage guidance.
How do I find an emergency vet when I am away from home?+
Use the AAHA (American Animal Hospital Association) hospital locator at aaha.org, the VCA Hospitals emergency finder, or simply search 'emergency vet near [city]' with your pet's current location. Save the address of the nearest 24-hour emergency animal hospital in your pet sitter's written instructions before you leave, not on the day of the emergency.
Can a pet sitter authorize veterinary treatment?+
A pet sitter cannot authorize treatment on your behalf unless you have provided written authorization. Before leaving, complete a veterinary authorization form that specifies a spending limit the sitter can approve without consulting you, and lists procedures they should discuss with you before proceeding. Most veterinary practices have their own consent forms for absent owners, but providing your own gives clearer parameters.
What should I include in my emergency pet care plan?+
Your plan should include: your primary vet's contact details and address; the nearest 24-hour emergency animal hospital; your pet's current medications, doses, and any known allergies; your pet's microchip number and insurance details; a signed veterinary authorization letter with a spending limit; the name and contact of at least two backup care contacts; and your contact details plus a secondary contact person who can make decisions if you are unreachable.
